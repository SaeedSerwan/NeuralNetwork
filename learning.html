How does a network learn?

The network starts with 13,002 random knobs and reads digits no better than a coin with ten sides. Learning is a loop: guess, measure how wrong the guess was, find which way to turn each knob, turn it a little. This page takes the loop apart: the cost function, gradient descent and backpropagation.

01Start random, then measure how bad it is

At the start, all 13,002 weights and biases are random numbers. Show the network a 3 and the output is garbage. To improve, we first need to say how bad it is. Compare each of the 10 outputs with what we wanted (1 for the correct digit, 0 for the others), square the differences and add them up: the cost of this one example.

\[ C = \sum_{j=0}^{9} \big(a_j - y_j\big)^2 \]

Before and after training

Pick a digit. Orange marks show what we wanted.

Random weights

After training (30 passes over 60,000 digits)

Large cost: wrong or unsure. Small cost: confident and right. The squaring makes every term positive and punishes big mistakes much more than small ones.

02The cost function: one number for the whole network

One example is not enough: a network that is perfect on this 3 may be terrible on every 7. So we average the cost over all 60,000 training digits:

\[ C_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} C_n \]

Now look at this as a function. The network takes 784 pixels and gives 10 numbers. The cost function takes the 13,002 weights and biases and gives one number: how bad the network is. The training data is fixed; the knobs are what change.

Output10 numbersone number: how bad the network is
Fixed13,002 weights and biasesthe 60,000 labelled training digits

Key idea: learning = make this one number as small as possible. Afterwards, test on 10,000 digits the network has never seen.

03Gradient descent: roll downhill

How do you find the lowest point of a function with 13,002 inputs? Start simple: imagine the cost depends on only one weight w. You cannot see the whole curve; you can only feel the slope under your feet. If the slope is negative, step right; if positive, step left. Make the step proportional to the slope: big steps on steep ground, small ones near the bottom.

\[ w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial w} \]

A ball on the cost curve

Click the curve to drop the ball. Then step, or run. Try a learning rate of 1.5.

Drop the ball at w = 3 and at w = −1. Where you start decides which valley you end in. The right valley is a local minimum: lower than its surroundings, but not the lowest point.

04With many weights: follow the negative gradient

With two weights, the cost is a landscape: contour lines join points of equal cost. The gradient is the arrow of slopes, one per weight; it points uphill, the steepest way. Go the opposite way. With 13,002 weights it is the same idea in 13,002 dimensions: nobody can picture it, but the arithmetic is identical.

\[ \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla C(\mathbf{w}) \]

The cost landscape of two weights

Click anywhere to start a ball. Darker = lower cost. Arrow: the downhill direction.

Momentum keeps part of the previous step, like a heavy ball, and can roll through shallow valleys. Mini-batch noise: real training estimates the gradient from 32 random digits at a time, so each step wobbles, but on average it still heads downhill.
Question

w = 2, gradient = 4, η = 0.1. New w? 2 − 0.1 × 4 = 1.6: the cost rises when w rises, so w goes down.

05What the gradient tells each knob

The gradient is not just a direction: it is one number per knob. Its sign says whether to nudge that weight up or down; its size says how much the cost cares about it right now. 06Backpropagation: computing the gradient

How do we get 13,002 slopes? Backpropagation. First the forward pass computes and stores every activation. Then, starting from the cost, we go backwards: a weight changes the cost only through a chain of effects, so we multiply the local slopes along the chain (the chain rule).

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial C}{\partial a} \]

In words: the output said “8” for a 3? Push the 8 down, push the 3 up, and pass the blame back to the neurons and weights that caused it. Follow it on a network small enough to compute by hand: 2 inputs, 2 hidden neurons, 1 output, all sigmoid, and we want the output to be 1.

Backpropagation, one number at a time

Blue dots: the forward pass. Orange dots: the backward pass. Press ▶ Play or go step by step; each round ends with a weight update and starts again.

Watch for: the orange numbers on the lines are the slopes ∂C/∂w. A negative slope means “increase this weight”; after the update step those lines get thicker (green) or less red. Round after round the cost falls.

In PyTorch all of this is one line, loss.backward(). But it is exactly these multiplications, done for 13,002 weights.

Key idea: backpropagation computes the gradient; gradient descent uses it. Cost says how bad. Gradient says which way. Learning rate says how far.

07Training our network

Put it together. Take a mini-batch of 32 digits, run the forward pass, compute the cost, backpropagate, take one step. Repeat for all 60,000 digits: one epoch. We trained for 30 epochs, about a minute on a laptop.

Why about 10% before training? Random weights pick one of 10 digits more or less at random, so they are right about 1 time in 10.
