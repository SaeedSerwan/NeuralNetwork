One recipe, different wiring.

Every network in this part uses the same neurons and the same gradient descent you met in Parts 1 and 2. What changes is how the neurons are connected, to fit the shape of the data: a table, an image, a sequence, or something new to create.

02CNN: small filters that slide over the image

Our dense network flattens the image into a list of 784 numbers and forgets which pixels are neighbours. Shuffle the pixels in the same fixed way for every image and it would learn just as well, although no person could read the result.

What a dense network “sees”

Both pictures contain exactly the same 784 numbers.

The digit

Same pixels, shuffled: a dense network finds both equally easy

A CNN keeps the picture as a grid. It looks at small patches with a filter, for example 3 × 3 weights, and uses the same 9 weights at every position:

\[ y_{i,j} = \sum_{u}\sum_{v} k_{u,v}\, x_{i+u,\,j+v} \]

Slide a filter over a digit

Press Play: the filter moves slowly at first so you can follow the numbers, then speeds up. Or hover the feature map to inspect any position. You can edit the filter weights.

Input 28 × 28

Feature map 26 × 26: green +, red −

A dense layer from 784 pixels to 16 neurons needs 12,544 weights. One 3 × 3 filter needs 9, and it finds its pattern anywhere in the picture.

A real CNN learns dozens of filters per layer instead of us choosing them, shrinks the maps with pooling, and stacks layers: edges → parts → objects. Here the “hope” of Part 1 really happens. On the same 28 × 28 digits a small CNN (LeNet) reaches about 99%, against our 95%.

03RNN: a network with memory

Text, speech and sensor readings come one element at a time, in any length. An RNN reads one word (or value) at a time and updates a hidden state h, its memory, using the same weights at every step:

\[ h_t = \tanh\big(W_h\, h_{t-1} + W_x\, x_t + b\big) \]

Reading a sentence, one word at a time

Each column of the heat map is the 8-number memory after one word.

Hidden state h after each word (8 numbers, −1 … 1)

How much of the first word is still in the memory

“This morning I took my cat for a ___” → walk. To predict the last word the network must still remember “cat” and “took … for a”. In a simple RNN the memory of early words fades step by step: the same vanishing gradient problem as in Part 4. An LSTM adds gates that decide what to keep and what to forget, so useful information can travel much further. For long text, transformers replaced RNNs.

04GAN: a forger and a detective

A GAN has two networks. The generator turns random noise into a fake; the discriminator looks at real and fake examples and says “real or fake?” (a digit reader with one output). Each trains against the other: the detective gets better at spotting fakes, so the forger must get better at making them.

\[ \min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big] \]

A GAN on one number

Grey: real data. Blue: the generator’s fakes. Orange curve: the discriminator’s D(x), its belief that x is real.

With two bumps, our tiny generator can only make one bump, so it ends up copying just one of them or sitting in between, and the detective keeps catching it. This is a toy version of mode collapse, one reason GAN training is called unstable.

Real GANs use deep networks for both players and produce faces, digits or artwork that never existed. Uses: synthetic training data, image editing, super-resolution. Risk: deepfakes. Cousins: autoencoders (compress and rebuild; badly rebuilt inputs are anomalies) and diffusion models (learn to remove noise step by step; behind most text-to-image systems).

05Transformers: attention

A transformer does not read one word at a time. Every word looks at every other word directly and decides how much each one matters to it: attention. Nothing has to survive a long chain of steps, so long texts are no problem. This is the architecture behind ChatGPT and Claude.
